    Section 2

    The leadership moment

    Most of you got here by being excellent problem-solvers. At senior level, an escalation tests something different: whether you own the outcome, not whether you can fix the ticket.

    Escalating isn't handing over

    When something goes up the line, it usually takes one of three forms. Only two of them are leadership.

    Raising a risk

    "Here's what's happening, what I've done and what could happen next. I'm still on it." You keep ownership and make sure the right people know early.

    Asking for a decision

    "Here are the options and my recommendation. I need you to decide X by Y." You keep ownership and bring a clear request.

    Passing the problem

    "We have a serious issue. Please advise." Nothing is contained or recommended, and nobody owns the next step. This is the one to avoid.

    Section 3

    Size it and decide

    Two mistakes are common at senior level: staying in the background when you should be front and centre, and taking over when your Manager should lead. Sizing the escalation first prevents both.

    Five questions to triage any situation

    Severity

    How bad is the impact now, and how bad could it get?

    Urgency

    How fast is it getting worse? Is there a deadline?

    Scope

    One team, one client, or several? Is it a repeat?

    Harm

    Is there a data, compliance, contractual or reputational risk?

    Continuity

    Could service delivery stop or fall over?

    Unknowns

    What don't you know yet? Missing facts aren't a reason to wait.

    Section 5

    Communicating with clients

    Clients forgive slow progress far more easily than silence or vagueness. Specific details calm people down.

    Build your holding statement

    Use this for your first message after an escalation, even before you know the root cause. It has four parts: Acknowledge, Own, Act, Next update.

    Which phrase builds trust?

    Choose the stronger option in each pair.

    Set an update rhythm, and keep it

    L2

    A daily update until it's stable.

    L3

    Twice a day, with a named contact.

    L4

    Agreed slots, executive briefings and a written log.

    Every update covers status against plan, what changed, risks and the next update time. If you have nothing new to say, send the update anyway, because a missed update can become a new escalation.

    Section 6

    Escalating to your own leaders

    Your CEO, Delivery Head or Director has very little time and needs to make a decision. A good upward escalation gives them everything in under 90 seconds, and keeps the ownership with you.

    Fix this escalation

    The decision-ready update

    1. What happened

    The facts, and when it started.

    2. Impact

    Who and what is affected now, and what could be next.

    3. Action taken

    What you've already done to contain it.

    4. Known and unknown

    What's confirmed, and what you're still checking.

    5. Recommendation and decision needed

    Your options and recommendation, the decision you need, from whom and by when.

    6. Owner and next update

    Who owns the next step and when they'll hear from you again.

    Across regions and time zones

    Our clients are mostly in the US, and our teams work from India, the Philippines and Sri Lanka. Escalations grow in the gaps between time zones, between shifts, and in the time it takes for bad news to reach you.

    The overnight gap

    Chennai (IST)9:00 amYour team logs on
    Colombo (SLT)9:00 amSame time as India
    Manila (PHT)11:30 amTwo and a half hours ahead
    New York (ET)11:30 pmThe night before

    During US daylight saving time. In US winter months, New York is 10:30 pm.

    A US client who emails at 2 pm Eastern may not hear back until it's almost midnight their time. To them, that silence looks like nobody cares. Agree who acknowledges escalations during the client's working day.

    Patterns to watch for

    These are tendencies people often describe, not rules about people. Every individual is different. Select a card to see the leader move.

    The handoff gap

    A high-priority issue is raised near the end of one region's shift. The next team assumes the previous team has told the client. The previous team assumes the next team has taken ownership. Six hours later, the client is still waiting.

    Make it safe to escalate early

    Set red lines in advance

    Agree which triggers must be escalated, by when and to whom, so nobody makes that call alone.

    Thank the messenger

    React to the news, not the person. Your first reaction decides whether you hear early next time.

    Reward amber, not just green

    Praise early warnings in public, even when they turn out to be minor.

    Fix first, review later

    Keep solving the problem separate from reviewing what went wrong. Blame stops people speaking up.

    Section 8

    When it gets worse

    Escalations rarely follow the plan. The first fix fails, stakeholders disagree, or someone more senior pushes for a shortcut. This is where leadership maturity shows.

    Section 9

    Prevent the next escalation

    The best senior leaders judge themselves on the escalations that never happen. An escalation isn't over when the client is calm. It's over when the cause is fixed and the client has heard what changed.

    Keep asking why

    The RCA says: "Agent keyed the wrong code." If a person's name is your root cause, you haven't finished. Select "Ask why" to dig deeper.

    Watch leading indicators

    • Backlog age and trend, not just volume
    • SLA or TAT dipping three days in a row
    • Share of new hires on live work
    • Client tone in calls and emails
    • Client or vendor changes in the last 30 days

    Run escalation governance

    • A weekly review across towers, not within one
    • Repeat escalation rate as your headline measure
    • Time to acknowledge, in the client's time zone
    • Preventive actions closed on time

    A rise in escalations isn't automatically bad news. When people feel safe to raise issues early, you may see more escalations at first, and fewer serious ones later. Watch repeats and severity, not just the count.

    STEER at a glance

    S: Stabilise (first hour)Contain the impact. Send a holding statement within the client's working hours.
    T: Take ownership (first hour)Own the outcome visibly. No blame, even if the cause sits elsewhere.
    E: Engage (first day)Client sponsor, your own leadership (before the client calls them), your team, and partner teams with a clear ask.
    E: Execute and communicate (until resolved)Run the recovery plan on a set update rhythm: L2 daily, L3 twice daily, L4 agreed slots.
    R: Review and prevent (after)Root cause (keep asking why), corrective and preventive actions with owners, pattern check, close the loop with the client.

    Cross-region handoff checklist

    • Named owner on the incoming shift
    • Current impact on the client
    • Actions completed so far
    • Open risks and anything that's getting worse
    • Next checkpoint, in the client's time zone
    • Who to escalate to, and how to reach them
    • Explicit acceptance: the incoming owner confirms "I've got it." No confirmation means no handoff.
